Dehalo

Models the wide, faint halos of bright stars and subtracts them from linear images. It keeps a natural halo near each star and removes the wings on the outskirts, where they cover galaxies, tidal tails and IFN. [more]

Keywords: halo, bright star, linear, point spread function, IFN, tidal tail, background

1 Introduction

[hide]

A bright star makes a wide halo. The light of a star does not stop at the core of the star image: the point spread function (PSF) has wide wings. They come from light that is scattered by dust and by small surface errors on the mirrors, lenses and filters, from diffraction, from internal reflections, and from scattering in the atmosphere (the aureole).

Thin clouds or haze can also make halos larger and add a wide glow around bright stars: the water droplets and ice crystals scatter the starlight, and when many subframes taken through thin clouds are stacked, this scattered light adds up in the master like a real part of the halo.

The halo can extend over thousands of pixels. It covers faint structures such as tidal tails, stellar streams and integrated flux nebula (IFN). Gradient tools do not remove it well, because the halo is a steep radial structure and not a smooth gradient.

Dehalo measures the azimuthal profile of each halo star, makes a smooth model of the halo, and subtracts a part of this model. The part that is subtracted changes with the distance from the star: near the star a small part is removed, so the star keeps a natural halo; far from the star all of the halo is removed.

Use the script on linear data only, before deconvolution and the stretch. The halo is additive only in linear data. A strong gradient can be corrected first with GradientCorrection: its protection of bright structures keeps the halos. A gradient tool without such protection can take a part of the halo into its model; then use Dehalo before that tool. The halo is not the same in different filters, so use the script on each master (L, R, G, B) or on a linear RGB image, where each channel gets its own model.

Section 6 gives the full model, the algorithms and the formulas.

2 Workflow

[hide]

  1. Open a linear image and make it the active window. Start the script.
  2. The script reads the image, finds the stars with a saturated core and fits their halos. You do not have to click a button. Each change of the image, the mask, the detection or the model settings starts a new fit after a short pause. The last result stays on the screen until the new one is ready.
  3. Optional: select an exclusion mask for the galaxy, tidal tails and IFN, so that these structures do not go into the halo model.
  4. Look at the star table. Untick the stars whose halo you want to keep. Remove false detections. Add missing stars with Shift+click in the preview.
  5. Set the reduction sliders. The preview shows the result immediately. Use Ctrl+click to compare before and after, and the hard stretch to see the faint halo and IFN.
  6. Click Apply.

3 Parameters

[hide]

The symbol after a parameter name is the symbol used in section 6.

3.1 Images

Target (linear): the image to process. A grayscale or an RGB image. Each channel of an RGB image gets its own model.

Exclusion mask: optional. Pixels where the mask is above 0.5 are not used for the fit: galaxy, tidal tails, streams, IFN. The mask is used only for the fit. The halo model is subtracted from all pixels.

Invert: use this for protection masks, where the structure is black. The script refuses a mask that excludes more than 80% of the image, because this is usually an inverted mask.

3.2 Halo stars

Binning ($B$): binning factor for the fit and the preview. The result is always calculated at full resolution.

Saturation ($t_{\mathrm{sat}}$): pixels at or above this value make a saturated core.

Min core r ($r_{\min}$): the smallest saturated core radius (px) that counts as a halo star. Use a lower value if a halo star is missing. RGB masters usually saturate less than L.

Max stars ($N_{\max}$): the largest number of detected stars, largest cores first.

Star table: one row for each star: position, core radius $r_{\mathrm{core}}$, fit radius $r_{\mathrm{fit}}$, tail slope $a_t$ and the noise radius $r_{\mathrm{cut}}$, where the profile goes into the noise. Manual stars have (m) after the core radius, edited detected stars (e).

  • Untick a star to keep its halo in the image. The star stays in the fit, so the models of the other stars still include its light.
  • Remove selected rows removes the selected stars from the model. Use it for false detections or wrong manual stars. Select rows with click, Ctrl+click or Shift+click in the table.
  • Shift+click core r: the core radius given to a star that you add with Shift+click in the preview. It sets the size scale of the star.

3.3 Model

Approach:

  • Empirical profile + power-law tail (default): the measured azimuthal profile, smoothed. It follows rings and bumps of the halo. Beyond the radius where the profile goes into the noise, the model continues as a power law.
  • Smooth power law (outskirts): one $A r^{-a}$ curve fitted on the outskirts. It is never above the measured profile. It is the smoothest model and it removes less near the star.

Fit inner radius (x core) ($f_{\mathrm{in}}$): the profile is measured from this multiple of the core radius.

Max halo radius (px) ($R_{\max}$): the halo radius of the brightest star. The radius of the other stars is scaled by their core radius. Beyond this radius the model decreases to zero over a further 50%.

Profile smoothing ($\lambda$): width of the window in log radius for the local power-law fit that smooths the profile (section 6.9).

Iterations ($N_{\mathrm{it}}$): number of joint fit iterations of all stars and the sky plane.

3.4 Reduction

These settings do not start a new fit. The preview changes immediately.

Outer reduction ($\alpha_{\mathrm{out}}$): the part of the halo that is removed far from the star. 1 removes all of the model.

Inner reduction ($\alpha_{\mathrm{in}}$): the part of the halo that is removed near the star. Low values keep more natural halo.

Core protection (× core r) ($c$, default 0): no subtraction inside $c\,r_{\mathrm{core}}$. The reduction increases smoothly to the full value at $2c\,r_{\mathrm{core}}$. The star core and the edge of the star stay as in the original image, so the star has no step or dark ring around it. 0 switches the protection off. The protection is off by default: set it together with the opacity, and start low, because a large zone leaves a bright glow around small stars.

Core protection opacity ($p$, default 0): how much of the core zone is protected. 1: nothing is subtracted inside $c\,r_{\mathrm{core}}$. Lower values also subtract a part of the halo in the zone, so a large protection radius does not leave a bright glow that ends abruptly around small stars. Values from 0.3 to 0.7 give a weaker glow that fades out. Near 0 the edge of the star becomes hard, because the model is constant inside the first measured ring (section 6.13).

Protect radius / Transition width (px) ($R_p$, $R_t$): the reduction changes smoothly from the inner value to the outer value between protect and protect + transition. The values are for the brightest star. In the preview, the yellow circle shows the protect radius and the green circle shows where the full outer reduction starts.

Scale radii with star brightness: scales the protect radius and the transition width of each star with its core radius ($\rho_s = \kappa_s$, else $\rho_s = 1$). The extent of the halo is approximately proportional to the core radius (section 6.5).

3.5 Apply options

Replace target image (default on): Apply writes the result into the target. The target gets one Dehalo (Script) entry in its history, and Undo restores the original image. Negative values are set to 0. After the replace, the script reads the target again and makes a new fit. A second Apply removes only the halo that is left.

The history entry contains all the parameters of the run: the dialog settings, the star list (position, core radius, ticked or not), the exclusion mask and the options. The History Explorer shows them. Drag the entry to the workspace to make a process icon, or onto an image to run Dehalo again with the same parameters and stars, without the dialog. For example, use the result on L for the R, G and B masters of the same field. The exclusion mask must be open with the same identifier.

When this option is off, Apply makes a new image <id>_dehalo. The target does not change. The new image gets the FITS keywords and the astrometric solution of the target.

Also create halo model image: Apply also makes <id>_halomodel, the part of the halo that was subtracted.

6 Theory and algorithm

[hide]

This section describes the model of the image, each step of the algorithm and the formulas that the script uses. Section 6.14 lists all symbols and the dialog parameters that set them.

6.1 Physical background

Far from the core, the PSF wings that section 1 describes (the aureole [2]) follow approximately a power law of the radius $r$:

$$\mathrm{PSF}(r) \propto r^{-a}$$

King measured a stellar profile over a range of 27 magnitudes and found $a \approx 2$ (an inverse-square law) in the outer wings [1]. The slope is different for each telescope, camera and sky, so the script does not assume a value: it fits $a$ for each star (sections 6.8 and 6.10).

Per pixel, the wing has only a very small part of the flux of the star, but it covers a very large area. For a star that saturates the sensor, the wing thousands of pixels from the star is still as bright as the outskirts of galaxies, tidal tails and IFN. For deep images of faint structures, the wings of bright stars are one of the main sources of false light [3, 4].

Light is additive: the linear image is the sum of the sky background, the objects and the halos. Thus, if we can estimate a halo, we can subtract it. After a non-linear stretch (for example the midtones transfer function), this is not true: the stretch mixes the halo and the signal, and a subtraction gives wrong results. For this reason the script works on linear data only.

The halo changes with the wavelength. Scattering and the aureole are stronger at short wavelengths, and the filters and the coatings change the reflections. For this reason each filter and each RGB channel gets its own model.

6.2 Image model

The script uses this model for one channel of the linear image $I$ at pixel position $\mathbf{x}$:

$$I(\mathbf{x}) = S(\mathbf{x}) + P(\mathbf{x}) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} H_k\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}_k\|\right) + n(\mathbf{x})$$
  • $S$: the signal that we keep: stars, galaxies, IFN, tidal tails.
  • $P$: a plane that approximates the large-scale background (section 6.12).
  • $H_k$: the halo profile of star $k$ with the centre $\mathbf{c}_k$. It is a function of the distance $r$ only.
  • $n$: noise.

The model has two assumptions:

  1. Each halo is azimuthally symmetric: it is the same in all directions from the star.
  2. In each ring around a star, most pixels are background. Objects cover only a small part of the ring. A robust statistic (the clipped median) rejects them, or the exclusion mask removes them.

The script estimates each $H_k$ and calculates the result

$$\label{output} I_{\mathrm{out}}(\mathbf{x}) = I(\mathbf{x}) - \sum_{k \in \mathcal{E}} w_k(r_k)\, H_k(r_k), \qquad r_k = \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}_k\|$$

where $\mathcal{E}$ is the set of ticked stars and $w_k$ is the reduction weight (section 6.13). The plane $P$ is not subtracted.

6.3 Binning

To make the fit fast, the script bins the image by $B$. For each channel it calculates the mean, and over all channels it calculates the maximum:

$$M(i,j) = \frac{1}{B^2}\sum_{u=0}^{B-1}\sum_{v=0}^{B-1} I(iB+u,\, jB+v)$$ $$M_{\max}(i,j) = \max_{c,\,u,\,v}\; I_c(iB+u,\, jB+v)$$

$M$ is used for the fit and for the preview. $M_{\max}$ is used to find saturated pixels. Binning decreases the number of pixels by $B^2$ (16 for $B=4$), and it decreases the noise per pixel by $B$. The centre of bin $(i,j)$ is at $\left((i+\frac{1}{2})B,\,(j+\frac{1}{2})B\right)$ in full-resolution pixels.

The halo changes slowly over a distance of $B$ pixels. The mean of a bin is the mean of the halo in the bin, so a model that is fitted on $M$ is correct at full resolution without a change of scale. All radii in the script are in full-resolution pixels.

6.4 Star detection

The saturated bins are the set $\{(i,j) : M_{\max}(i,j) \ge t_{\mathrm{sat}}\}$. The script finds the connected areas of this set with a flood fill (4-connectivity) [12]. An area of $n$ bins has the area $nB^2$ pixels. The core radius is the radius of a circle with the same area:

$$r_{\mathrm{core}} = \sqrt{\frac{n B^2}{\pi}}$$

The core radius is a measure of the brightness of the star: the brighter the star, the larger the radius where the profile goes above the saturation level. Areas with $r_{\mathrm{core}} < r_{\min}$ are rejected. The remaining stars are sorted by $r_{\mathrm{core}}$ and the first $N_{\max}$ are kept.

The centre is the centroid of all saturated full-resolution pixels (all channels) in a square of half size $2r_{\mathrm{core}}+16$ around the binned position:

$$\mathbf{c} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{I_c(\mathbf{p}) \ge t_{\mathrm{sat}}} \mathbf{p}$$

A saturated core is flat, so this centroid is the geometric centre of the core. The centre must be precise: an error $\delta$ of the centre gives a residual of approximately $\delta \, \frac{dH}{dr}$, which is positive on one side of the star and negative on the other side.

A manual star (Shift+click) has its centre at the click position and its core radius from Shift+click core r. To correct a star, select it (Space in the preview or its row in the table), move it with Alt+drag and change its core radius with the + and − keys or Ctrl+wheel. This works for detected stars too, for example when a stacking artefact or a neighbour star moves the centre of the saturated core. An edited detected star becomes a manual star, marked (e), and keeps its detected position. When the stars are detected again, manual and edited stars stay, and detection does not add a star near the detected position of an edited star. To go back to the detected values, remove the row and change a detection setting. A manual star that you added does not block detection, so a small star next to a bright one does not remove the bright star.

A star outside the frame can have a halo that reaches into the image. Zoom out below the fitted view, then Shift+click at the star's position outside the image, or Alt+drag a star there. Set its core radius so that the model has the size of the halo. The profile uses only the pixels in the image; rings without enough pixels are not used. A detected star keeps its tick state if a previous star is nearer than $3\max(r_{\mathrm{core}})$ of the two stars.

6.5 Star geometry

The detected star with the largest core is the reference; for an edited star its detected core radius counts. Manual stars that you add are not used for the reference, so adding or resizing one does not change the other stars. If there is no detected star, the largest manual star is the reference. For each star $s$:

$$\kappa_s = \frac{r_{\mathrm{core},s}}{\max_{j\,\in\,\mathrm{detected}} r_{\mathrm{core},j}}$$ $$r_{\mathrm{in},s} = \max\left(f_{\mathrm{in}}\, r_{\mathrm{core},s},\; 2B,\; 8\right)$$ $$r_{\mathrm{fit},s} = \max\left(R_{\max}\, \kappa_s,\; 20\, r_{\mathrm{in},s}\right)$$

The profile is measured between $r_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $r_{\mathrm{fit}}$. Inside $r_{\mathrm{in}}$ the pixels are saturated or near saturation, where the sensor is not linear, so they are not used.

Why the radii are proportional to the core radius: if the profile is $A r^{-a}$, the core radius is the radius where the profile is equal to $t_{\mathrm{sat}}$, and the halo radius is the radius where the profile is equal to a small level $\varepsilon$:

$$r_{\mathrm{core}} = \left(\frac{A}{t_{\mathrm{sat}}}\right)^{1/a}, \qquad r_{\varepsilon} = \left(\frac{A}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/a} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{r_{\varepsilon}}{r_{\mathrm{core}}} = \left(\frac{t_{\mathrm{sat}}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/a}$$

The ratio does not depend on the flux $A$. Thus, for stars with the same slope $a$, the halo radius is proportional to the core radius. This is an approximation: near the core the real profile is not a pure power law.

6.6 Exclusion map

A bin is not used in any fit if one of these conditions is true:

  • $M_{\max} \ge 0.9\, t_{\mathrm{sat}}$: saturated or almost saturated pixels, where the sensor is not linear.
  • The exclusion mask is above 0.5 in one or more pixels of the bin. The mask is binned with the maximum, after the optional inversion $m \to 1-m$.
  • The distance to the centre of a halo star is less than $\max(3 r_{\mathrm{core}},\, r_{\mathrm{in}})$. Thus the core of one star does not go into the profile of another star.

Small stars without saturation are not masked. In each ring they are a small number of high values, and the clipped median rejects them.

6.7 Azimuthal profile

The profile is measured in rings with logarithmic spacing:

$$e_0 = r_{\mathrm{in}}, \qquad e_{b+1} = \max\left(1.035\, e_b,\; e_b + B\right), \qquad \text{until } e_b \ge r_{\mathrm{fit}}$$

Each ring is 3.5% of its radius wide, and at least one bin wide. Near the star the profile changes quickly, far from the star it changes slowly. Equal steps in $\ln\, r$ give the same relative resolution at all radii. The radius of ring $b$ is the geometric mean $r_b = \sqrt{e_b\, e_{b+1}}$.

For star $k$, the values in ring $b$ are the residuals after the plane and the models of all other stars are subtracted:

$$v_p = M_p - P(\mathbf{x}_p) - \sum_{j \ne k} H_j\left(\|\mathbf{x}_p - \mathbf{c}_j\|\right), \qquad e_b \le \|\mathbf{x}_p - \mathbf{c}_k\| < e_{b+1}$$

Only bins that are not excluded are used. The value of the ring is a sigma-clipped median [6]. The scale $\sigma$ comes from the 15.87% and 84.13% quantiles. For a normal distribution these are $\pm 1\sigma$ [7], and they do not change much when there are outliers:

$$\sigma = \frac{1}{2}\left(q_{0.8413} - q_{0.1587}\right)$$

The script repeats these steps, at most 6 times: calculate the median $m$ and $\sigma$ of the values that are kept, then keep only the values in $[m - 2.5\sigma,\; m + 2.5\sigma]$. It stops when no value is removed or when fewer than 5 values are left. The values are sorted one time; each clipping step is a window of the sorted values that two binary searches find. If a ring has more than 5000 values, the script uses 5000 values at a fixed step. A ring with fewer than 30 values is empty.

The standard error of the median of $n$ values from a normal distribution is [8]:

$$\varepsilon_b = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\;\frac{\sigma_b}{\sqrt{n_b}} \approx 1.2533\,\frac{\sigma_b}{\sqrt{n_b}}$$

Why the median: stars, galaxies and IFN filaments are positive outliers in a ring. If most of the ring is background, the median moves very little. The mean would follow the outliers, and the objects would go into the halo model.

6.8 Local sky offset and tail fit

The plane is only an approximation of the background, so near each star a constant offset $s$ can remain. Far from the star, a faint halo and a constant offset look almost the same. Thus the script fits both together on the outer part of the profile, $r \in [r_{\mathrm{last}}/6,\; r_{\mathrm{last}}]$, where $r_{\mathrm{last}}$ is the outermost ring with data:

$$p(r) = s + A\left(\frac{r}{1000}\right)^{-a}$$

For a fixed slope $a$ the model is linear in $s$ and $A$. With $x_b = (r_b/1000)^{-a}$, the least-squares solution comes from the 2×2 normal equations [9]:

$$A = \frac{n\sum x_b p_b - \sum x_b \sum p_b}{n \sum x_b^2 - \left(\sum x_b\right)^2}, \qquad s = \frac{\sum p_b - A \sum x_b}{n}$$

The script tries $a = 0.60, 0.62, \ldots, 4.00$ and keeps the solution with $A > 0$ and the smallest sum of squared residuals. A grid search is robust: the problem is not linear in $a$, and the residual can have more than one minimum. The halo profile is then $h_b = p_b - s$. The reference radius of 1000 px only keeps $A$ in a good numeric range.

6.9 Smoothing and noise radius

The profile is smoothed with a local linear regression [13] in $u = \ln\, r$. For each ring $b$, a straight line is fitted to the rings near it, with Gaussian weights:

$$\min_{\beta_0,\,\beta_1} \sum_j g_{bj} \left(y_j - \beta_0 - \beta_1 (u_j - u_b)\right)^2, \qquad g_{bj} = \exp\left(-\frac{(u_j-u_b)^2}{2\sigma_u^2}\right), \qquad \sigma_u = \sigma_s \ln\,1.035, \qquad \sigma_s = 1 + 6\lambda$$

and $\tilde h_b = \beta_0$. The sum is over $|u_j - u_b| \le 3\sigma_u$ and skips empty rings. Where all the rings in the window are significant ($h_j > 3\varepsilon_j$), the fit uses $y_j = \ln\, h_j$ and $\tilde h_b = e^{\beta_0}$. A power law is a straight line in $(\ln\, r, \ln\, h)$, so it goes through the smoothing without change. In the noise, the fit uses $y_j = h_j$, because $\ln\, h$ is not defined for values near 0.

The window has a fixed width in $\ln\, r$. Near the core the rings are only $B$ pixels wide (section 6.7), so a window with a fixed number of rings would be very wide in $\ln\, r$ there. A moving average over such a window makes the steep inner profile flatter: too low near the core and too high just outside it. Subtraction of a model that is too high makes a dark ring around the star. The local linear fit in $\ln\, r$ does not have this error.

The smoothed profile is never more than the measured median plus two standard errors, and the rings inside the first measured ring get its value:

$$\tilde h_b \leftarrow \min\left(\tilde h_b,\; h_b + 2\varepsilon_b\right)$$

The window averages approximately $\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_s \approx 2.5\,\sigma_s$ rings, so the noise of the smoothed profile is approximately:

$$\tilde\varepsilon_b = \frac{\varepsilon_b}{\sqrt{2.5\,\sigma_s}}$$

The noise radius $r_{\mathrm{cut}}$ is the radius where the profile goes into the noise. Going outward, the script looks for 3 rings in sequence with $\tilde h_b < 2\tilde\varepsilon_b$. $r_{\mathrm{cut}}$ is the last ring before them with $\tilde h_b \ge 2\tilde\varepsilon_b$ (at least the 4th ring). Beyond $r_{\mathrm{cut}}$ the measured profile is only noise, and the model uses the power-law tail (section 6.10). The column Noise r in the star table shows $r_{\mathrm{cut}}$.

6.10 Halo model

Tail. A power law is fitted to the smoothed profile on $[r_{\mathrm{cut}}/3,\; r_{\mathrm{cut}}]$, as a straight line in log-log space (ordinary least squares on the rings with $\tilde h_b > 0$):

$$\ln\, \tilde h_b = \ln\, A_t - a_t \ln\, \frac{r_b}{1000}$$

If there are not sufficient points, the script uses the slope from section 6.8, or $a_t = 2$. The slope is limited to $0.5 \le a_t \le 5$. Then the amplitude is set so that the tail continues the profile at $r_{\mathrm{cut}}$ without a step:

$$A_t = \tilde h(r_{\mathrm{cut}}) \left(\frac{r_{\mathrm{cut}}}{1000}\right)^{a_t}$$

The column Tail slope in the star table shows $a_t$.

Empirical model (default). Between the rings, the profile is interpolated linearly in $\ln\, r$. Negative values are set to 0:

$$H_{\mathrm{emp}}(r) = \begin{cases} \tilde h_0, & r \le r_0 \\ \tilde h_b + \dfrac{\ln\, (r/r_b)}{\ln\, (r_{b+1}/r_b)}\left(\tilde h_{b+1} - \tilde h_b\right), & r_b < r \le r_{b+1} \le r_{\mathrm{cut}} \\ A_t\left(\dfrac{r}{1000}\right)^{-a_t}, & r > r_{\mathrm{cut}} \end{cases}$$

Smooth power-law model. One power law $A_p r^{-a_p}$ is fitted in log-log space on $[r_p,\; r_{\mathrm{cut}}]$, with $r_p = \max(r_{\mathrm{in}},\; \frac{1}{2}\rho R_p)$. The model is never above the empirical model:

$$H_{\mathrm{PL}}(r) = \min\left(H_{\mathrm{emp}}(r),\; A_p\left(\frac{\max(r,\, r_{\mathrm{in}})}{1000}\right)^{-a_p}\right)$$

A power law that is fitted on the outskirts is too steep near the core. Without the minimum, it would be larger than the real halo near the star and the subtraction would make a dark hole.

Taper. Beyond $r_{\mathrm{fit}}$ the model decreases smoothly to zero at $1.5\,r_{\mathrm{fit}}$:

$$H(r) \leftarrow H(r)\left[1 - \operatorname{smoothstep}\left(\frac{r - r_{\mathrm{fit}}}{0.5\, r_{\mathrm{fit}}}\right)\right], \qquad \operatorname{smoothstep}(t) = 3t^2 - 2t^3, \quad t \text{ clipped to } [0,1]$$

The derivative of smoothstep [14] is zero at both ends, so the model has no edge in the image.

The script stores the model in a lookup table with one value for each full-resolution pixel of radius, and interpolates linearly between the values. Thus the subtraction at full resolution is fast.

6.11 Joint fit of all stars

Halos overlap. The rings of a faint star near a bright star contain the halo of the bright star. If each star were fitted alone, the halo of the bright star would also go into the model of the faint star, and the sum of the models would remove too much light. Thus the script fits all stars and the plane together with backfitting (block coordinate descent, Gauss-Seidel order) [5, 15]. $T$ is the sum of all halo models on the binned grid:

T ← 0
P ← plane fit of M
repeat N_it times:
   for each star k:
      T ← T − H_k                                  remove the model of star k
      measure the profile of M − P − T around c_k     section 6.7
      H_k ← halo model from the profile                sections 6.8 to 6.10
      T ← T + H_k
   P ← plane fit of M − T                             section 6.12

Each step finds the best value of one block (one star or the plane) while all other blocks stay the same. The fit of each star sees only the light that the other stars do not explain. For halos that overlap a little or a medium amount, 2 to 3 iterations are sufficient. The default is 3.

Unticked stars are part of $T$. They are fitted, but they are not subtracted from the result. Each channel of an RGB image has its own fit.

6.12 Sky plane

$$P(\hat x, \hat y) = \alpha + \beta \hat x + \gamma \hat y, \qquad \hat x = \frac{x}{W} - \frac12, \qquad \hat y = \frac{y}{H} - \frac12$$

$W$ and $H$ are the image width and height. The normalized coordinates keep the equations well conditioned. The plane is fitted on the bins that are not excluded, on the values $v = M - T$:

  1. Start: $\alpha$ is the clipped median (section 6.7) of each 7th bin, $\beta = \gamma = 0$, and $\sigma$ is its clipped scale.
  2. Three times: use each 2nd bin in $x$ and $y$; reject bins with $\left|v - P\right| > 3\sigma$; solve the normal equations [10], then set $\sigma$ to the RMS of the residuals that are kept:
$$\left(\sum \mathbf{f}\,\mathbf{f}^{T}\right)\mathbf{\theta} = \sum \mathbf{f}\, v, \qquad \mathbf{f} = (1,\, \hat x,\, \hat y)^{T}, \qquad \mathbf{\theta} = (\alpha,\, \beta,\, \gamma)^{T}$$

The 3×3 system is solved with Gauss-Jordan elimination with partial pivoting [11].

The plane only helps the fit: without it, a gradient would go into the halo profiles. The plane is not subtracted from the result. You must still remove the gradient (DBE, MultiscaleGradientCorrection, GraXpert). This is easier after the halos are removed, because a halo is not a smooth gradient.

6.13 Reduction weight and output

The part of the halo that is removed changes with the distance from the star:

$$w_k(r) = \alpha_{\mathrm{in}} + \left(\alpha_{\mathrm{out}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{in}}\right)\operatorname{smoothstep}\left(\frac{r - \rho_k R_p}{\rho_k R_t}\right)$$

For $r \le \rho_k R_p$ the weight is $\alpha_{\mathrm{in}}$. For $r \ge \rho_k (R_p + R_t)$ it is $\alpha_{\mathrm{out}}$. Between these radii it changes smoothly. The core protection $c$ and its opacity $p$ multiply the weight by a second factor:

$$w_k(r) \leftarrow w_k(r)\left[1 - p\left(1 - \operatorname{smoothstep}\left(\frac{r}{c\, r_{\mathrm{core},k}} - 1\right)\right)\right]$$

With $p = 1$ the factor is 0 for $r \le c\, r_{\mathrm{core},k}$ and 1 for $r \ge 2c\, r_{\mathrm{core},k}$; with $p < 1$ it is $1-p$ in the zone. In this zone the result is $I(1 - w_k)$ with $w_k$ increasing outward, so the profile of the star falls monotonically and has no step. With the default $c = 3$, the protected zone is the zone that the fit excludes (section 6.6), where the model has no measurements. The result at full resolution is equation \eqref{output}: $I_{\mathrm{out}} = I - \sum_k w_k H_k$, for each channel and each pixel.

  • Near the star, $(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{in}})\,H_k$ stays in the image. This is the natural halo that the eye expects around a bright star. Low $\alpha_{\mathrm{in}}$ also keeps parts that the azimuthal model cannot describe (spikes, ghosts) inside a natural halo.
  • Far from the star, where the halo covers faint structures, $\alpha_{\mathrm{out}} = 1$ removes all of the model.
  • $\alpha_{\mathrm{out}} > 1$ removes more than the model. Use it only if the model is too low; it can make a dark ring.
  • Inside the first measured ring the model is constant. With $c = 0$ this constant is subtracted from the core and the edge of the star; the default core protection prevents this.

The preview view Removed halo shows $\sum_k w_k H_k$. With Replace target image, $I_{\mathrm{out}}$ is written directly into the target (one Script entry in the history) and negative values are set to 0.

7 Limits

[hide]

  • Azimuthal model only. Diffraction spikes, radial streaks, reflection ghosts that are not centred on the star, and halos that are not round (sensor tilt, collimation errors) stay in the image as lobes. Keep $\alpha_{\mathrm{in}}$ low, so that they stay inside a natural halo.
  • Large objects without a mask. If an object covers a large part of a ring, the median includes it, and a part of the object goes into the halo model. Use an exclusion mask for the galaxy, tidal tails and bright IFN.
  • Extrapolation. Beyond $r_{\mathrm{cut}}$ the halo is below the noise of one ring and the model is an extrapolated power law. Max halo radius limits how far the extrapolation goes.
  • Very wide halos and gradients. Far from the star, a very wide halo and a large-scale gradient are almost the same. The plane and the offset $s$ take a part of this light, so the far wing can be a little too low.
  • Only stars with a saturated core are detected. Add the other stars with Shift+click. If the master is normalized so that saturated stars are below 0.95, decrease Saturation.
  • Radial streaks and spikes. The model is an azimuthal median. Near a very bright star the halo can be made of radial streaks; after the subtraction the streaks stay a little brighter and the gaps between them a little darker than the sky. A lower inner reduction keeps these parts inside a natural halo.
  • Deconvolution after Dehalo. Deconvolution (for example BlurXTerminator) makes any step or dark ring around a star stronger. Keep the core protection on when you deconvolve after Dehalo.
  • Linear, unprocessed data only. Do not use the script after a stretch, and preferably not after deconvolution or noise reduction, because these change the profile of the halo.
